## 1. Agile Team Charter

**Evidence:** **[View Agile Team Charter](../../assets/AgileTeamCharter_PRP370S-1.pdf)**.

**Reflection**

Writing the charter at the start forced the team to agree on how we would work before we built anything. The values I leaned on most were *Transparency* and *Continuous Improvement*. Our daily stand-ups, the shared Trello board and the logbook all came straight from the charter's communication plan. The three stand-up questions (what I completed, what I plan to do, what is blocking me) kept small problems, such as the Figma permission issue and the font licensing question, visible and solved within a day instead of growing.

Looking back, the charter was also optimistic in places. It set a 9-week timeframe and listed "no physical hardware prototype" as a constraint, but our backlog later grew to 15 sprints and included beta units and firmware. My charter role (Frontend Developer and UI Designer) also shifted in practice. In the logbook I worked mainly as Writer/Editor on copy, brand documents and proofreading, while the design work sat with Thapelo. If I did this again, I would treat the charter as a living document and revisit roles and constraints at each retrospective instead of leaving them unchanged.

**Connects to:** the logbook (the ceremonies it records) and the product backlog (the scope that outgrew the 9-week plan).

## 2. User Stories and Tasks

**Evidence:** **[Trello Boards](../../assets/WaterWatch_Product_Backlog.pdf)**.

**Reflection**

Writing stories from the user's point of view kept our decisions tied to real people. The persona, Thandi Ndlovu, and her pain points (surprise bills and undetected leaks) shaped the screen copy and the brand tone. Breaking each story into four day-sized tasks made progress easy to see in stand-ups, and it let me hand work over cleanly, for example UX copy to Thapelo for the Figma prototype.

There were weaknesses. Our stories had no acceptance criteria, priorities or estimates, so "done" was judged by team agreement instead of a written test. The cards also listed all four members, which made individual contributions hard to separate. I would add acceptance criteria to every story and assign each task to one person. We had a short disagreement on how granular tasks should be. We settled it by keeping the user-story-plus-tasks format we had used since Sprint 1, which kept the backlog consistent.

**Connects to:** the product backlog (where the stories live) and the sprint backlog (where they become tasks).

## 3. Product Backlog

**Evidence:** Screenshot or PDF export of the Trello board (`WaterWatch_Product_Backlog.pdf`): 57 cards across Sprints 1 to 15, labelled Day 1 to Day 4.

**Reflection**

I helped configure the product backlog with the team. We sequenced the epics and stories through to project close, wrote testable descriptions, and cross-checked everything against Deliverable 2 so it still matched our accepted feasibility plan. I presented the backlog on 24 July and explained how items were prioritised. It was accepted as presented.

The backlog did its main job of giving the team a clear order of work. Its weakness was scope. Planning all 15 sprints up front gave a complete picture, but in practice we worked only until the app was fully functional (Sprint 7 Day 1, beta units loaded with the final app build). The later sprints, such as the pilot, data analysis and final report, were not carried out, and the board does not yet show that clearly. I updated the board to mark completed work and moved the unworked sprints into a descoped list so it reflects reality. The lesson is that a backlog should be refined as we learn, with only the near-term sprints planned in detail.

**Connects to:** the charter (the 9-week plan the backlog outgrew) and the logbook (the record of what we actually completed).

**Reflection**

The sprint backlog was where the product backlog became something I could act on each day. Taking one story and splitting it into four days of tasks made the sprint goal concrete, and the board showed at a glance what was done and what was left. Sprint 6 worked well because the tasks followed a sensible order. We tested the payment flow before confirming the pilot, and we confirmed the pilot before we printed signage.

What I would improve is the detail. Our cards named tasks but gave no estimates and no owner per task, so I added owners here from the logbook. Giving each task an owner and an estimate on the board would make workload and delays easier to see. This sprint also showed how dependent the work was on other people. I held back some sections until the video scripts were locked, and I waited on review comments. Seeing those dependencies in a sprint backlog earlier would have helped us sequence tasks better.

**Connects to:** the product backlog (where these tasks came from) and the logbook (the daily record of the same sprint).

## 5. Agile Logbook

**Evidence:** `WaterWatch_Logbook_Marco_Updated.docx` (Sprints 3 to 7, 14 July to 18 August 2026), exported to PDF or linked.

**Reflection**

The logbook turned the three stand-up questions into a daily habit. Writing down what I completed, what I planned and what blocked me made my own progress visible and gave the team a shared record. Reading it back shows a clear pattern. Early sprints were about brand identity, tone of voice and the prototype, then the product backlog and presentation, then the pre-order flow and pilot preparation, and finally video production and loading the final app build.

The impediments I recorded were mostly small and solved quickly. They included Figma access, a disagreement on brand tone settled by team vote, a font licensing question settled by switching to a free-for-commercial alternative, and review comments arriving late. This confirmed the value of raising problems early, as our charter says. I also noticed gaps. The sprint numbering in the logbook does not always match the Trello sprint names, and the logbook has no formal retrospective notes. In future I would add a short retrospective at the end of each sprint and keep the sprint names identical on both.

**Connects to:** the charter (the stand-up and retrospective ceremonies) and the sprint backlog (the tasks each entry reports on).

## Overall connection

Together the five artefacts show one cycle. The charter set how we would work, the user stories said what we would build and for whom, the product backlog ordered the work, the sprint backlog turned it into daily tasks, and the logbook recorded what actually happened. The main thing I learned is that the plan needs to be kept in step with the work. Where our documents drifted apart (the 9-week charter against a 15-sprint backlog, and a board that lagged behind the logbook), the cause was the same: we did not revisit the plan often enough.
